## How to mark

Wrap the words in double square brackets:

    This is [[a testament to]] the team.

If you want to say why, add a note after a bar. Notes are optional but useful:

    [[It wasn't about the technology. It was about the people. | fake contrast]]

Rules of thumb:

- Mark what bothers you, not what a rule says should. Your taste is the answer key.
- A tell can be one word, a phrase, a whole sentence or a pattern across sentences.
  For a pattern, mark the clearest example of it.
- A sample with nothing to mark is a valid answer. Write `CLEAN` on the verdict line.
- Do not fix the text. Only mark it.
- Do not open `key.json` until you are done. It says where each sample came from,
  and knowing that would bias you.

Under each sample there is a verdict line. Fill in one of: `CLEAN`, `A FEW TELLS`,
`OBVIOUSLY AI`.

## s04

In post No. 3, I covered the AI limitations that are expiring, but there's one in particular that seems to have staying power. In that same Stanford report, AI judges favoring their own answers was one of only three findings (out of 50) that got stronger in newer models.

My take on why: part of it is obvious. A review in the same session reuses the reasoning that produced the work. (It's like proofreading your own essay: you read what you meant to write, not what's on the page.) The other part (less obvious, and lower risk) is familiarity. Models rate text higher when it sounds like something they'd write.

One mitigation for this is simple. Ask the agent session you're in to spin up a fresh, read-only sub-agent (a second AI helper that can look but not touch). It starts with a clean slate (no memory of your conversation) and one job: review the work.

## s10

Choosing an AI tool for your team can feel overwhelming. There are hundreds of options, and new ones launch every week. So where do you start?

Start with the problem, not the product. Identify one task that is repetitive, time-consuming, and low-risk. Test two or three tools against that task. Then compare the results on quality, speed, and cost.

This approach has several benefits. It keeps the evaluation focused. It gives you real evidence instead of marketing claims. And it builds confidence across the team before a larger rollout.

The right tool is the one your team will use. Everything else is secondary.

## s26

In my experience, fresh reviewers regularly catch what the original session missed: logic gaps in writing and recommendations, and bugs that would've broken the code. Sub-agent tip: give each one a narrow job, have it rank findings by severity, and include a "no issues found" option so it doesn't invent problems. (Because remember… they like to suck up.)

Bonus for more advanced users: if you have specific types of reviews you do often or want the agent to look for certain things, you can create pre-defined sub-agents that will execute more consistent reviews. (Think a fact-checker, a security reviewer, or a skeptical client.) Just ask your agent how to do this.

Fresh "eyes" are helpful, but they also have a blind spot. A new reviewer doesn't know which judgment calls you may have made on purpose, so it may try to re-litigate them. Treat its feedback as a list of suggestions, not a to-do list.
